\title[Mapping a research area with AI agents]{A map of the range, not a way
  up the wall: six weeks of AI agents on 373 open problems in analysis}
\author{Øistein Søvik}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
For six weeks, one mathematician with a master's degree directed AI coding
agents at every open problem that could be found in one area of analysis:
Hardy, Bergman and Dirichlet-series spaces, Hankel and Helson operators, and
Nehari-type theorems. The agents collected 373 problems, found that 64 of them
had already been settled by others, and produced answers to 141 more. We
classify these answers by how they were obtained and by how much they matter.
Most are easy in hindsight: short arguments from known tools, results assembled
from the literature, or counterexamples that are easy to check once found. Six
are, in our judgement, of real significance. On the hardest problems, days of
work by several agents produced maps of what fails, not proofs. We conclude
that current agents are very good at mapping a field and clearing its long
tail, and that a hard problem stays hard. All results of ours are sketches
until they appear on arXiv or in a journal; this is a living document, updated
as the people who asked the questions respond.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Terence Tao has described research mathematics as a range of mountains:
\begin{quote}
  Tao gave the analogy of mathematicians trying to climb ``a big mountain
  range with lots of tall mountains and lots of foothills''. Humans can only
  climb one step at a time, but they can plan a route to the top of a
  mountain like Everest. Meanwhile, Tao said, current AIs are like jumping
  robots. They can sometimes ``parkour their way to the top of a 6-foot
  wall'' that a human couldn't climb. But they can't do long-term strategic
  planning. Those 6 feet might become 10 feet, or 100, Tao imagines, but
  ``the little jumping robots are nowhere near the Mount Everests of
  math''~\cite{quanta}.
\end{quote}
This paper is a field report on what such robots do when pointed at an entire
range. Over six weeks in autumn 2026 we used Claude Code and Codex agents to
find, sort and attack every open problem we could locate in one research area.
The agents found 373 problems in papers, problem lists and monographs. They
established that 64 were already solved, and they produced answers to 141
others.

The answers need careful reading. Our main finding is that the gain was in
cartography, not in climbing:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The agents were very good at mapping a field: finding problems,
        classifying them, checking the literature, and clearing problems that
        were easy but had received little attention.
  \item Most of their answers are low-hanging fruit. Of the 141, we classify
        6 as significant, 62 as useful, and 73 as minor.
  \item On problems where experts had made partial progress and stopped,
        the agents produced reformulations, special cases and catalogues of
        obstructions, not proofs.
\end{itemize}
These numbers need to be read with care. We did not solve 141 problems in the
sense a mathematician would mean. The database mixes interesting questions from
the field, questions at its research frontier, and questions we derived
ourselves; many of the answers are to the last kind, or to questions that
turned out to be easy once someone looked. Of almost 400 problems, about six of
real significance were solved. What the agents added beyond that is partial:
special cases and correct orders of growth for 142 problems, reductions of some
25 problems to three common hard cores, and written records of why each
attempted route failed. These are new insights into the problems, not solutions
of them.

Breadth explains the counts. Throw a hundred balls at a hundred baskets and
some go in; that is useful, and different from getting better at the shot that
matters. Tao's description of current tools as ``artificial general
cleverness''~\cite{tao-cleverness} fits what we saw.

We therefore present this method as the first step of a research project, not
the last: a fast way to get to know a field and to find questions worth working
on. \Cref{sec:map} describes how the problems were found and sorted,
\cref{sec:workflow} the workflow, \cref{sec:results} the results and their
classification, \cref{sec:walls} the hard problems, and \cref{sec:verification}
verification and credit. \Cref{sec:related} places the work among other
reports, and \cref{sec:limits} lists its limits.

\section{Setting}

The area is the theory of Hardy and Bergman spaces on the disc and on the
infinite polydisc, Dirichlet series viewed as functions on the infinite torus,
and the Hankel, Helson and composition operators acting on them. A typical
question asks for the sharp constant in an inequality, whether an extremal
function can have zeros, or whether a bounded bilinear form has a bounded
symbol. The active literature is a few hundred papers, much of it from a small
number of research groups.

One person directed the work. He chose targets, judged whether a result
mattered, steered agents that were stuck, read everything before it went out,
and handled all contact with other mathematicians. The agents were Claude Code
(Claude Opus 5.5 as the main session, with smaller Claude models for cheap
subtasks) and Codex (several GPT models), often running at the same time in the
same repository. The record of the work is the version history, with about 1000
commits, together with the logs: 14 Claude Code sessions with 393 subagents,
and 586 Codex sessions, 420 of them subagents.

\section{Building the map}\label{sec:map}

The problems came from four sources, in this order.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Stated problems in a local paper collection.} Agents searched
        each paper for environments labelled Conjecture, Question, Problem or
        Open problem, and for phrases such as ``remains open'' and ``it would be
        interesting to''. Each candidate was recorded with the paper, the exact
        location, and a quote.
  \item \emph{The papers those cite}, with the same extraction.
  \item \emph{Synthesis passes.} Agents read the recent papers of the core
        authors ``between the lines''. This produced \emph{implicit} questions,
        which a remark suggests without posing, and \emph{inferences}: paper A
        proves one statement and paper B another, so a third should follow.
  \item \emph{Problem lists and monographs}: workshop problem sessions, a
        classical problem book, and the open-problem sections of two monographs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{From candidates to 373 records}
\Cref{tab:funnel} follows the problems from extraction to the database. The
local collection was broad: the director's paper library also held books from
unrelated projects, so the first catalogue contained questions from fluid
dynamics, arithmetic geometry and combinatorics alongside the target area.
Scope was then fixed by one sentence: \emph{Hardy and Bergman spaces, Dirichlet
series, analytic extremals, Hankel operators, sampling and interpolation, and
directly related analytic number theory}, informally ``what the people working
in this area would work on''. An agent applied it theme by theme and wrote down
a reason for every excluded theme, for example ``Euler and Navier--Stokes
questions are outside the selected scope'' or ``sphere-packing and
energy-optimality conjectures are excluded''; adjacent topics were kept only
with a stated connection, such as random multiplicative functions for their
link to Dirichlet-polynomial norms. Incomplete candidates, mostly exercises,
were dropped. Excluded records were kept in a separate report rather than
deleted.

Each problem became one Markdown file with validated metadata. The metadata
records:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the theme, the kind (298 stated, 55 implicit, 20 inferences) and the
        origin (289 asked by others, 84 by us);
  \item tags, an impact score and a difficulty score;
  \item a dated literature check with quoted evidence;
  \item the stage reached in our own work;
  \item once anyone settled the problem, how far the resolution is trusted:
        journal, arXiv, or our own.
\end{itemize}
Moving from a single catalogue file to one file per problem let several agents
and two different tools edit the database at once.

\subsection*{How difficulty was scored}
Each problem received a difficulty score from 1 to 5, in two rounds. On 10 and
11 October, 194 problems from the first catalogue were assessed by cheap Codex
agents (GPT-6 Luna), each with about ten minutes: a literature check, a short
attempt, and a ``speculative difficulty score and confidence'' on the scale 1 =
routine deduction or literature reconciliation, 2 = focused tractable project,
3 = substantial specialist project, 4 = probably requires a major new idea, 5 =
longstanding frontier problem. Their brief warned that ``a ten-minute failure
to prove a statement is not evidence for a high score''. On 12 October three
Claude Opus 5.5 subagents scored the whole database, each taking a group of
themes, under a common brief that defined difficulty as ``effort for us with
current tools'' and fixed an anchor problem at each level:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{cl}
    \toprule
    Difficulty & Meaning                                                                \\
    \midrule
    1          & short argument from known tools; days                                  \\
    2          & focused effort; an existing method adapts, or a computation settles it \\
    3          & needs a genuinely new idea, but a route is visible                     \\
    4          & hard; the natural approaches are known to fail                         \\
    5          & major open problem, or equivalent to one                               \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each score came with a one-line justification. The agents read each record's
statement, literature status and evidence, and the source where the statement
was unclear; resolved problems were scored as originally posed. For the 189
problems that already had a score from the first round, the Opus agents kept it
unchanged; they scored the rest from scratch. Over all 373 problems the initial
scores were distributed as 37, 48, 110, 145 and 33 for difficulty 1 to 5. The
scores moved during the work. After the scoring pass, 145 of the 168 problems
that remained unsettled were re-rated, almost always upwards (127 by one level
and 23 by two), as attempts failed. Most of these moves were mechanical: the
protocol raised any problem that used its session budget without a full answer
to difficulty 5 (see below). Of the 141 problems our agents settled, 38 were
re-rated, mostly downwards. The final scores therefore record the outcome
rather than predict it, and we use the initial scores of 12 October throughout.
Even these are not blind: they were assigned after the directed work of 3 to 11
October, so for problems attempted then the score already reflects that
attempt.

\subsection*{Selection for attack}
Problems were attacked in rounds by difficulty, from 1 upwards: ``start with
the low-hanging fruit''. Within a round the orchestrating session picked open,
unparked problems with the best impact relative to difficulty, mixing cheap
problems with one or two of high impact, and skipping problems whose literature
status was an unverified claim by others. Each problem had a session budget
equal to its difficulty, from one session at difficulty 1 to four at difficulty
4; difficulty 5 problems were worked only in dedicated rounds with a specific
new idea. The protocol then moved problems mechanically: a problem at
difficulty 1 to 4 that used its budget without a full answer was raised to
difficulty 5, and a problem at difficulty 5, or after six sessions without even
a partial result, was parked. Parked problems could be reopened only for a new
idea, paper or tool.

The public problem volume was a further selection: questions asked only
internally were removed, leaving 308 questions and 58 answers.

\section{The workflow}\label{sec:workflow}

Each problem was worked in sessions. A session begins by reading the record,
the source passage and the log of earlier attempts, and by writing a three-line
plan. It ends by rewriting a short \emph{state of play} and appending one line
to the \emph{attempt log}: the route, the outcome, the reason, and the next
step. A claimed result had to become a \emph{solution sheet}: the problem as
posed, the answer as a theorem, a complete proof with exact citations, and
nothing else, built through a lint-gated LaTeX pipeline. A fresh agent with a
fixed brief then refereed it. The referee re-read the question in its source,
re-derived each step, reran any certificate, and decided whether the answer was
full or partial.

On hard problems several agents worked in parallel \emph{lanes}, each on one
approach, with a coordinating session redirecting them. When lanes drifted into
cataloguing obstructions, a \emph{prove-mode} brief told each to commit to one
strategy and push it to a theorem, or to a proof complete except for one
precisely stated lemma. The full instructions are published with this paper as
a reusable set of agent playbooks.

\subsection*{The record format, and what it was worth}
The database replaced a set of catalogue files and a shared ranking that
several workers appended to at once. Two failures forced it: agents repeated
routes that had already failed, because the failures lived only in chat
transcripts, and two tools with several subagents each needed to edit the list
concurrently. Most fields were added one at a time over the following day, each
answering a request from the director: a difficulty score ``to give us some
starting points'', ``different levels of solved'' (the resolution tiers), a way
to say which papers bear on a problem, and, a day later, whether we or someone
else had asked the question.

To see which fields mattered we counted, over all 772 commits that touched the
database, how many changed each field.
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lr}
    \toprule
    Field                                    & Commits changing it \\
    \midrule
    State of play (prose)                    & 615                 \\
    Attempt log (prose)                      & 566                 \\
    Stage                                    & 532                 \\
    Session count and date                   & about 400           \\
    Failed approaches (prose)                & 322                 \\
    Related papers                           & 319                 \\
    Artifacts                                & 304                 \\
    Literature evidence                      & 275                 \\
    Review                                   & 273                 \\
    Full or partial                          & 230                 \\
    \midrule
    Origin, impact, tags                     & 1--12               \\
    Authors told                             & 3                   \\
    Claim basis, abandonment reason, aliases & 4 each              \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
The fields that carried the work were the prose memory between sessions and the
few fields that move a problem through the pipeline. Scores, tags and origin
were filled once in bulk. Two lessons were negative. The free-text
justification of the scores was rewritten in 426 commits, because escalations
were appended to it, and so it went stale. The field recording that the askers
had been told was changed in only three commits, because nothing in the
pipeline required it. Other fields that nothing in the pipeline depended on,
such as a free-text relevance line and the minutes spent, were also hardly ever
filled in. The lesson is that the prose memory between sessions, together with
the handful of fields that move a problem through the pipeline, did the real
work; a field that nothing reads or checks is not kept up to date. The field we
most missed was how the source poses the question, which we had to reconstruct
for \cref{sec:results}. The schema we now recommend keeps the working fields,
freezes the score justification at triage and logs escalations in the attempt
log instead, adds the form of the question, a verbatim quote, the reviewing
model and a frozen initial difficulty, makes the publishing step refuse a
result whose askers have not been told, and drops the unused fields.

\subsection{What the literature check found}

The difficulty assigned to a problem before anyone attempted it predicted the
outcome well (\cref{tab:difficulty}). It also showed why easy problems are
easy: nine of the twelve problems first rated easiest already had a published
answer. Tao observed the same correlation on the Erdős problems: the problems
current tools can solve are ``precisely the category of nominally open problems
that are most likely to have been solved in the
literature''~\cite{tao-lowhanging}. The literature check also caught one
problem recorded as resolved by a paper that had since been withdrawn.

\subsection{What kind of results were these?}

We classified each of the 141 answers along three axes.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Sign}: 82 proofs, 42 counterexamples, and 17 determinations
        of a value, set or order.
  \item \emph{Kind}, meaning how the answer was obtained:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item 20 assembled from the literature;
          \item 58 routine new arguments;
          \item 44 explicit constructions or certificates, which are the work
                to find and easy to check;
          \item 19 substantial new arguments.
        \end{itemize}
  \item \emph{Significance}:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item 6 high: paper-level answers to someone else's question;
          \item 62 medium: full answers worth a short note;
          \item 73 low: our own questions, conditional or elementary answers,
                and corollaries.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
The classification is ours and preliminary; \cref{tab:sign} gives the
cross-tabulations.

Three things stand out.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Counterexamples are the natural strength.} Thirty-nine of the
        42 counterexamples are explicit constructions or certificates. Searching
        a space of candidates and checking each one is exactly what agents do
        well, and what earlier work on machine-assisted discovery exploited
        \cites{wagner,funsearch,alphaevolve}.
  \item \emph{Few proofs are substantial.} Only 17 of the 82 proofs needed
        a genuinely new argument; the rest are routine or assembled from the
        literature.
  \item \emph{The significant results needed direction.} Of the six results
        we rate high, four are proofs and two are counterexamples. Three of the
        six came from focused sessions in which the human directed one problem
        with the asker's question in view, not from the bulk campaign over the
        database.
\end{itemize}

The six high-significance results are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item that extremal functions for bounded linear functionals on $H^p$,
        $0<p<1$, are outer, answering a published conjecture in a stronger
        form, written jointly with the asker;
  \item a sharp bound on the number of zeros of extremals in a
        prescribed-coefficient problem;
  \item the finite-exponent limits of sharp Bernstein--Nikolskii constants;
  \item the exact order of growth of the $H^1$ norms of partial-sum
        operators for Dirichlet series;
  \item a counterexample to a positive-symbol Nehari question, with a
        computer certificate in outward-rounded arithmetic;
  \item that optimal polynomial approximants in $H^p$ can vanish in the
        disc for every $p\neq2$.
\end{itemize}
Each has been sent to the people who asked the question, or is being prepared
for them. None is yet on arXiv.

\subsection{How the question was posed}
The database mixes questions of very different standing: conjectures, where an
author predicts the answer; numbered problems and questions, including those
from problem sessions and monographs; informal remarks such as ``it would be
interesting to know''; and questions we derived ourselves. The records did not
store this distinction, so we recovered it afterwards from each record's source
location; the split is approximate.

    Question (others)        & 67      & 12            & 24        & 31        & 2    \\

is significant. All six significant results answer a conjecture, problem or
question that someone else had posed formally. Informally posed questions were

record the form of each question when it is harvested.

\subsection*{Outer extremals}
The first claim came after the director told the agent that its numerical
sweeps did not get closer to a proof:
\begin{quote}
  I believe I've proved [the nonvanishing conjecture of Brevig and Saksman]: for $0<p<1$, every extremal for $C(k,p)$ has no zeros in the disc. [\dots] Only I have checked the proof, plus numerics at $k=2$; no one else has.
  \hfill---Claude Opus 5.5, 8 October, 13:56
\end{quote}
Two hours later a subagent extended it from one coefficient to every bounded
linear functional, including singular inner factors. The next evening a Codex
agent estimated that a Lean formalization would take ``months, potentially
longer''; it was finished overnight:
\begin{quote}
  Done---the full theorem is now Lean-verified: every extremal for a nonzero bounded complex-linear functional on $H^p$, for every real $0<p<1$, is outer.
  \hfill---GPT-6.1 Sol, 10 October, 00:54
\end{quote}

\subsection*{At most one zero}
A Codex subagent found the key step; the deciding idea was to fix one
perturbation phase rather than average over phases:
\begin{quote}
  The full real-direction Hessian yields a direct contradiction for any extremal with at least two zeros. [\dots] I checked the projections, the cancellation of the [\dots] terms, and admissibility of the perturbation. I found no gap in the argument.
  \hfill---GPT-6 Luna, 8 October, 20:49
\end{quote}
The idea was relayed to the Claude session within minutes, which then reported
that it had ``reached the same conclusion independently''. We cannot verify
that claim, and we credit the Codex subagent.

\subsection*{Zeros of optimal polynomial approximants}
This story took one evening. At 21:58 the director asked how close the agents
were to the full result:
\begin{quote}
  We're not close to the full result if that means interior zeros for every $p\neq2$. What we have is solid for $p$ close to 1, and nothing yet for $p$ near 2 or above it.
  \hfill---Claude Opus 5.5, 10 October, 21:58
\end{quote}
Twenty-seven minutes later a subagent reported ``there is no obstruction'', and
the main session wrote:
\begin{quote}
  Outer targets now work for $p>2$ as well. Agent A found the construction, and I checked it independently. So for every $p\neq2$ there is an outer target (even a polynomial one) whose degree-one OPA has a zero in $\mathbb D$.
  \hfill---Claude Opus 5.5, 10 October, 22:25
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Bernstein--Nikolskii limits}
\begin{quote}

\section{What the agents did not settle}\label{sec:unsettled}

Full answers are only half the picture. Of the 373 problems, 168 remain
unsettled: 142 with partial results of ours and 26 without. We compared them
with the 141 settled ones; the counts below come from the records, and the
classification of obstructions is our judgement after reading each record's
state of play.

\subsection{How they differ}
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lrr}
    \toprule
                                                         & Unsettled (168) & Settled (141) \\
    \midrule
    Initial difficulty 4 or 5                            & 126             & 35            \\
    Asked by others                                      & 83\%            & 63\%          \\
    Impact 4 or more                                     & 19\%            & 4\%           \\
    Partial progress by others already in the literature & 50\%            & 21\%          \\
    Answer is a counterexample (or expected to be one)   & 5\%             & 30\%          \\
    Inferences of ours                                   & 1               & 16            \\
    Mean number of working sessions                      & 1.8             & 1.5           \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
The problems that resisted were not neglected: they received slightly more
working sessions than the settled ones, and only four received none. They
differ in kind. They were asked by others, they matter more, half of them had
already been attacked in the literature, and they ask for a characterisation,
an exact constant or a uniform estimate rather than a single construction. The
settled problems, by contrast, include most of our own derived questions and
most of the problems a counterexample could close. Themes rooted in number
theory and probability (random multiplicative functions, Bohr radii, general
Dirichlet series, Paley--Wiener extremals) were mostly unsettled; composition
operators and the extremal problems around one published conjecture were mostly
settled.

\subsection{What blocked them}
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lr}
    \toprule
    Main obstruction                                               & Records \\
    \midrule
    Only special cases or families proved                          & 59      \\
    Needs an estimate that is itself a known hard problem          & 37      \\
    Open-ended, ambiguous, or in need of reformulation             & 23      \\
    Endpoint or critical case; every method loses a log or a power & 22      \\
    Exact constant wanted; only bounds or expansions found         & 14      \\
    Numerics suggest the answer, but no route to a proof           & 6       \\
    Depends on unrefereed or unavailable literature                & 5       \\
    Not attempted in depth                                         & 2       \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
About 25 records reduce to three underlying problems: an $L^1$ endpoint
estimate behind Schatten-class Nehari theorems, the value of one sharp constant
in a multiplicative Hilbert-type inequality, and the unknown extremal functions
of point evaluation in Paley--Wiener spaces. Settling any one of these would
move many records at once; none moved.

\subsection{Full versus partial}
A full answer settles the question as posed; a partial one does not. Of the 142
partial results, the main type was:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lr}
    \toprule
    Type of partial result                                      & Records \\
    \midrule
    Special case, subrange or family                            & 90      \\
    Counterexample to a stronger statement or a literal reading & 15      \\
    Correct order, or two-sided bounds, without the constant    & 13      \\
    Reduction to an equivalent reformulation                    & 12      \\
    Conditional on a hypothesis or an unrefereed preprint       & 7       \\
    One direction only                                          & 5       \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
A recurring kind is a local result near a point where the problem is already
solved, for example near $p=2$, which proves the conjecture in a neighbourhood
but gives no uniform control away from it. This is the pattern the human
director called ``filling $\mathbb R$ with $\mathbb Q$'': more and more special
cases, none of which converges to the general statement.

\section{Walls}\label{sec:walls}

We attacked several problems on which experts have made partial progress, for
days and with several agents. Two examples, described without the technical
details:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{An endpoint Schatten-class Nehari problem.} Every route the
        agents found reduced to one square-function estimate in $L^1$ that is a
        theorem in its own right. They proved many special cases, wrote down
        equivalent reformulations, and catalogued exactly where each route
        failed. They did not find a proof, and the problem was handed back to
        the person who asked it.
  \item \emph{A contractive restriction conjecture.} The agents proved the
        conjecture for two natural families of functions and refuted a stronger
        statement that would have implied it. The general case did not move.
\end{itemize}
In both cases the output was useful as a map. It is not progress on the wall
itself, and we do not present it as such. The pattern matches Tao's image: some
walls of six feet were climbed; the Everests were not approached.

\section{Verification and credit}\label{sec:verification}

Producing a candidate proof was cheap; checking it was the bottleneck. This
matches Tao's analysis of the problem-solving pipeline, in which generation is
followed by verification, exposition, acceptance and absorption into the
standard theory, and in which AI threatens ``proof
indigestion''~\cite{tao-age}. Our answers were checked by agent referees, in
some cases by a second model family, and in a few cases by Lean formalization
or exact-arithmetic certificates. Agent referees caught real errors: a bound
used outside its range, a missing hypothesis, and above all overstated scope,
where an answer to a special case was reported as an answer to the question.
They rarely asked whether the claim was the right one.

For this reason every result of ours is labelled a \emph{sketch} until it
appears on arXiv or in a journal, however it was checked. Before anything is
posted, the people who asked the question are told; this follows the
recommendations of the Leiden Declaration on attribution, disclosure and the
humanity of authorship~\cite{leiden}. The questions belong to those who asked
them. The proofs and computations were produced by AI agents and checked by the
author. Following Tao's rule of thumb~\cite{tao-age}, no result should be
published until a human can give a clear, expert-level talk on it.

\section{History and files}\label{sec:infrastructure}

\emph{Version control was the research log, but only once we used it.} The
repository was managed with Jujutsu (\texttt{jj}), which recorded 2409
operations and 1007 commits. Of the operations, 1944 fall on 12 and 13 October,
after the rule that each problem record is committed together with the work it
describes. The directed work of 3 to 11 October, which produced four of the six
significant results, left only 136 operations; those results can be dated only
by when their write-ups first appeared. Everything quantitative in this report
(when problems were settled, how difficulty scores moved, which record fields
were used) comes from the fine-grained part of the history. Jujutsu's automatic
snapshots meant that no edit by an agent was ever lost, and cheap small commits
kept changes per record. Our playbooks therefore default to Jujutsu with a git
backend, so that git-based hosting keeps working, and require a commit per
problem from the first day.

\emph{Failed attempts sprawled.} The rule that TeX files hold results only,
with failures recorded in one line of the problem record, was adopted but not
enforced. The research folders ended with about 350 Markdown notes, 575 TeX
files, 260 scripts and 140 data files. 107 problem records list five or more
artifacts, and one hard problem accumulated 31 files. Parallel agents each
wrote their own note instead of appending to a shared one, superseded drafts
were archived rather than deleted, and exploratory scripts were saved next to
certificates. Later agents had to read through the pile to avoid repeating
routes, and sometimes did not. The playbooks now require one notes file per
problem with dated sections, scratch work outside the repository, scripts in
the repository only when a claim depends on them, and deletion of superseded
drafts, which the history keeps.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection*{Agents on lists of open problems}
Several groups have run AI systems over whole lists of open problems in the
past year, and their numbers are the natural comparison for ours
(\cref{tab:compare}).
\begin{table}[ht]
  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    Study                                & Problems               & Outcome                              \\
    \midrule
    Gemini on Erdős problems~\cite{feng} & 700 open               & 13 addressed                         \\
    Formal proof search~\cite{tsoukalas} & 353 Erdős; 492 OEIS    & 9; 44 resolved                       \\
    OEIS Open~\cite{adamczewski}         & 492 OEIS               & 147 resolved                         \\
    Provers and verifiers~\cite{leduc}   & 66 conjectures         & about a third                        \\
    QED~\cite{qed}                       & 18 expert-chosen       & 5 original works                     \\
    FAR pipeline~\cite{far}              & 4717 harvested         & 598 candidate resolutions            \\
    UQ~\cite{uq}                         & 500 unsolved questions & 15\% pass validation                 \\
    This report                          & 373 harvested          & 64 known; 141 claimed; 6 significant \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Reported outcomes of AI systems run on lists of open problems.
    The lists differ widely in difficulty, and so do the meanings of
    ``resolved''.}\label{tab:compare}
\end{table}
The closest study to ours is the Gemini case study on the Erdős problems, which
reached the same conclusion: ``the `Open' status of the problems was through
obscurity rather than difficulty''~\cite{feng}. Many of the problems it
addressed were already solved in the literature, as were nine of our twelve
easiest problems. The authors of the OEIS study note that their conjectures
``are of uncertain mathematical significance, and most have likely received
little previous attention''~\cite{adamczewski}. The FAR pipeline~\cite{far} is
the nearest match to our method, at about fifteen times our scale: it extracted
6453 candidate problems from 5245 combinatorics papers and sent 77 potential
resolutions to their authors. Like us, its authors identify the selection of
problems and the review of the results as the bottlenecks. An early small test
with five easy conjectures found the same pattern as ours: the easy ones fell,
and the one that needed two papers combined did not~\cite{godel}. Individual
resolutions from such sweeps have been written up with
care~\cites{sothanaphan,alexeevshort}, and one Erdős prize problem thought open
for half a century turned out to have been settled three decades before it was
posed~\cite{alexeevmixon}.

\subsection*{Harvesting problems}
Problem lists are now being built automatically. DeepMind's formal conjectures
collect 2615 statements in Lean, including 1029 open conjectures; AI proofs and
disproofs were used to find badly stated problems~\cite{formalconj}.
OpenConjecture extracts conjectures from new arXiv papers with a language model
and ranks them by apparent tractability, with the caveat that the results
``have not been independently verified''~\cite{openconjecture}. FrontierMath's
open-problems pilot sets out to provide a ``denominator'' for AI
contributions~\cite{fmop}, and MathArena tracks performance on questions
extracted from recent papers~\cite{matharena}. The Erdős problems
database~\cite{erdos} and its AI wiki~\cite{erdos-wiki} remain the closest
analogue to our database; the wiki warns that ``absence of past progress may
reflect obscurity rather than difficulty''.

\subsection*{Mathematicians' experience}
Gowers writes that ``if a problem can be solved by means of a relatively
standard argument, it is highly likely that an LLM will be able to find and use
that argument'', while its suggestions on harder problems often ``sound
promising until I think about them carefully''~\cite{gowersblog}. That is our
experience in one sentence. Case studies from
industry~\cites{woodruff,comathematician} and from individual groups, including
one in functional analysis~\cite{banachlattices} and one with a consumer
model~\cite{vibe}, report the same division of labour: models search for
proofs, and experts close them. Earlier work on machine-assisted discovery
found constructions and counterexamples by
search~\cites{davies,wagner,funsearch,alphaevolve,georgiev}, and experiments
with frontier models as research assistants are collected in~\cite{bubeck}.
Formal proof~\cites{polu,htps,alphageometry,mathlib,avigad} and
evaluation~\cites{frieder,collins,frontiermath,hle,firstproof} form the
background.

\subsection*{Verification and significance}
Bloom, who maintains the Erdős problems database, reports ``a wave of
AI-produced solutions provided with no explanation'', and that much recent
progress came from renewed human effort ``rather than just
AI''~\cite{bloomchanges}. Kallel and El Louadi separate the validity of a
derivation, which a proof checker settles, from whether the formal statement
means the intended question and whether the result matters; ``only the first is
mechanizable''~\cite{kallel}. This is our distinction between settled and
significant. A survey of arXiv mathematics finds substantive AI use growing to
about 14\% of submissions by August 2026, concentrated in combinatorics rather
than analysis~\cite{goldrush}, and long agent runs are documented to drift in
the ways we saw~\cite{leanmarathon}. On the goals of mathematics beyond problem
solving we follow Thurston~\cite{thurston}, Gowers~\cite{gowers},
Tao~\cites{tao-good,tao-age} and Bessis~\cite{bessis}; journalism on the
current state includes~\cite{quanta}, where Igor Pak notes that language models
are ``fantastic in finding the right references''.

\section{Limits}\label{sec:limits}

This is one person's experience in one area, over six weeks, without a control
group. The difficulty and significance scores were assigned by the same agents
and by the author, not by independent experts. Correctness has not been audited
on a random sample; we intend to do so, and the counts above should be read as
counts of claimed answers. The models will be superseded within months.
Finally, the area has a dense literature of recent preprints, which made the
literature check unusually productive.
