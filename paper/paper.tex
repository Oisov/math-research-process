\title[Mapping a research area with AI agents]{Mapping a research area with AI
  agents: a workflow, and 373 open problems in analysis}
\author{Øistein Søvik}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
We describe a workflow that lets a mathematician with graduate-level
familiarity with an area enter its research literature and make research-level
progress with AI coding agents, and we report what it produced in one area of
analysis: Hardy, Bergman and Dirichlet-series spaces, Hankel and Helson
operators, and Nehari-type theorems. The workflow has four parts: a harvested
map of the area's open problems, explicit rules for directing agents, a
per-problem record that carries the work from one session to the next, and
review gates before anything leaves the repository. We describe each part as it
was used, the failures that changed it, and the version we now recommend. In
October 2026 the agents collected 373 problems, found that 64 had already been
settled by others, and produced claimed answers to 141 more. Most of these are
easy in hindsight. Seven are, in our judgement, of real significance, and on
the hardest problems the agents produced maps of what fails rather than proofs.
A blind audit of a random sample of 20 answers found no mathematical errors,
but two that answer less than was asked. The work was directed by one person
with a master's degree in the area who is not a research specialist. All
results of ours are sketches until they appear on arXiv or in a journal, and
the next stage is review by the people who asked the questions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Terence Tao has described research mathematics as climbing ``a big mountain
range with lots of tall mountains and lots of foothills''~\cite{quanta}. Human
mathematicians climb one step at a time, but they can plan a route to a distant
summit. In his picture, current AI tools are small jumping robots: they can
sometimes ``parkour their way to the top of a 6-foot wall'' that a human could
not climb, but they cannot plan the long route, and ``the little jumping robots
are nowhere near the Mount Everests of math''~\cite{quanta}.

This paper is about the person who points the robots. Our claim concerns a
method: with a workflow of the kind we describe, someone with graduate-level
familiarity with an area, but without a specialist's command of any of its
problems, can enter the research literature, find out what is open, and make
progress that a specialist would recognise as research. We give the workflow in
enough detail to be reused, the rules the author used to direct it, the ways it
failed and how each failure changed it, and what it produced in one area of
analysis in October 2026.

The area is the theory of Hardy and Bergman spaces on the disc and the infinite
polydisc, Dirichlet series viewed as functions on the infinite torus, and the
Hankel, Helson and composition operators acting on them. A typical question,
and one of the results discussed below, comes from Brevig and
Saksman~\cite{brevigsaksman}. For $0<p<1$ the Hardy space $H^p$ consists of the
analytic functions $f$ on the unit disc for which
$\sup_{r<1}\int_0^{2\pi}\abs{f(re^{it})}^p\,\dd t/2\pi$ is finite; it is not a
Banach space, and many standard tools fail. Among all $f$ in the unit ball of
$H^p$, consider those that maximise the size of the $k$th Taylor coefficient.
Brevig and Saksman conjectured that every such extremal function has no zeros
in the disc. The conjecture is precise and checkable, it had resisted its
authors' methods, and an answer would be noticed by the few dozen people who
work on such problems. Most questions in our database are of this kind.

The outcome, in brief: the agents collected 373 problems, established that 64
were already solved, and produced claimed answers to 141 others. Most answers
are short arguments from known tools, results assembled from the literature, or
counterexamples that are easy to check once found. Seven are, in our judgement,
significant. On the problems where experts had made partial progress and
stopped, the agents produced special cases, reformulations and records of what
fails. The gain was largest in mapping the area and clearing its long tail, and
smallest on its hardest problems.

\section{The starting point}\label{sec:start}

Three kinds of competence are easy to conflate.\@ \emph{Familiarity with a
field} means knowing its objects, standard tools and main results well enough
to read a recent paper.\@ \emph{Command of a problem} means knowing what has
been tried on it, why each attempt failed, and which estimate would decide it;
this is what a specialist brings.\@ \emph{Directing research} means choosing
what to work on, recognising when an argument is progress and when it is churn,
and judging whether a result matters. The author had the first: a master's
thesis on Nehari's theorem for Dirichlet series (NTNU, 2017) and no research
career in the area since. He had the third only in the weak sense of having
watched it done. He had the second for no problem in the database.

The month before the campaign built the first kind of competence back up and
produced its raw material. In August and September the author used the same
agents to grow the thesis into a monograph, in three editions, and an online
book on a teaching website. This writing did three things for the later work.
It forced a shared vocabulary between author and agents, so that a brief could
say ``the Helson-matrix route'' and mean one thing. It left a local collection
of about 300 papers, which became the first source of problems. And it produced
mathematics: one of the seven significant results, the critical case of a
conjecture of Bondarenko, Brevig, Saksman and Seip~\cite{bbss}, was first
proved as a theorem of the monograph on 11 October, a day before the problem
database existed, and only later recognised as the answer to a stated
conjecture.

The agents were Claude Code (Claude Opus 5.5 as the main session, with smaller
Claude models for cheap subtasks) and Codex, with OpenAI models that Codex
offered under the names GPT-6 Luna, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol, often running
at the same time in the same repository; neither tool could message the other,
so the author relayed between them. The research phase ran from 3 to 14 October
2026. Its record is the version history, with about 1000 commits, and the
session logs: 14 Claude Code sessions with 393 subagents, and 586 Codex
sessions, 420 of them subagents.

\section{Building the map}\label{sec:map}

The problems came from four sources, in this order.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Stated problems in the local paper collection.} Agents searched
        each paper for environments labelled Conjecture, Question, Problem or
        Open problem, and for phrases such as ``remains open'' and ``it would be
        interesting to''. Each candidate was recorded with the paper, the exact
        location, and a quote.
  \item \emph{The papers those cite}, with the same extraction.
  \item \emph{Synthesis passes.} Agents read the recent papers of the core
        authors ``between the lines''. This produced \emph{implicit} questions,
        which a remark suggests without posing, and \emph{inferences}: paper A
        proves one statement and paper B another, so a third should follow.
  \item \emph{Problem lists and monographs}: workshop problem sessions, a
        classical problem book, and the open-problem sections of two monographs.
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{tab:funnel} follows the problems from extraction to the database. The
local collection was broad, since the author's library also held books from
unrelated projects, so the first catalogue contained questions from fluid
dynamics, arithmetic geometry and combinatorics. Scope was then fixed by one
sentence: \emph{Hardy and Bergman spaces, Dirichlet series, analytic extremals,
Hankel operators, sampling and interpolation, and directly related analytic
number theory}, informally ``what the people working in this area would work
on''. An agent applied it theme by theme and wrote down a reason for every
excluded theme, for example ``Euler and Navier--Stokes questions are outside
the selected scope''; adjacent topics were kept only with a stated connection,
such as random multiplicative functions for their link to Dirichlet-polynomial
norms. Excluded records were kept in a separate report rather than deleted.

Each problem became one Markdown file with validated metadata: its theme, its
kind (298 stated, 55 implicit, 20 inferences) and origin (289 asked by others,
84 by us), tags, impact and difficulty scores, a dated literature check with
quoted evidence, the stage reached in our own work, and, once anyone settled
the problem, how far the resolution is trusted. \Cref{app:record} shows a
complete record. Moving from a single catalogue file to one file per problem
let several agents and two different tools edit the database at once.

\subsection*{Difficulty as a tool for ordering work}
Each problem received a difficulty score from 1 to 5. The scores were a device
for deciding what to attack next and how long to persist, and we read them that
way; they are not calibrated predictions. They came in two rounds. On 10 and 11
October cheap Codex agents (GPT-6 Luna), each with about ten minutes per
problem, assessed 194 problems from the first catalogue: a literature check, a
short attempt, and a ``speculative difficulty score''. Their brief warned that
``a ten-minute failure to prove a statement is not evidence for a high score''.
On 12 October three Claude Opus 5.5 subagents scored the whole database under a
common brief that defined difficulty as ``effort for us with current tools''
and fixed an anchor problem at each level:

    Difficulty & Meaning                                                                \\
    \midrule

    2          & focused effort; an existing method adapts, or a computation settles it \\
    3          & needs a genuinely new idea, but a route is visible                     \\
    4          & hard; the natural approaches are known to fail                         \\
    5          & major open problem, or equivalent to one                               \\

For the 189 problems that already had a first-round score, the Opus agents kept
it. Over all 373 problems the initial scores were distributed as 37, 48, 110,
145 and 33 for difficulty 1 to 5. Scores then moved as attempts failed, mostly
by a mechanical rule (\cref{sec:directing}), so the final scores record
outcomes; we use the initial scores of 12 October throughout. Even these are
not blind, since they were assigned after the directed work of 3 to 11 October.

\section{Directing the work}\label{sec:directing}

The author's part of the work was a small set of decisions, made again and
again. We state them as rules, as they were eventually written into the agents'
instructions.\@

\emph{What to attack.} Problems were attacked in rounds by difficulty, from 1
upwards: ``start with the low-hanging fruit''. Within a round the orchestrating
session picked open, unparked problems with the best impact relative to
difficulty, mixing cheap problems with one or two of high impact, and skipping
problems whose literature status was an unverified claim by others.\@

\emph{How long to persist.} Each problem had a budget of working sessions (one
agent attempt, typically up to an hour) equal to its difficulty. A problem at
difficulty 1 to 4 that used its budget without a full answer was raised to
difficulty 5, and a problem was \emph{parked}, removed from the queue, once it
reached difficulty 5 or had used six sessions without a partial result.
Difficulty-5 problems were worked only in dedicated rounds with a specific new
idea. Budgets first counted minutes; they were changed to sessions on the same
day, because agents given 30 minutes used two to four and stopped, so time was
not the binding constraint.\@

\emph{When to reopen.} A parked problem could be reopened only for a new idea,
a new paper or a new tool, and the reopening had to name it. One contractivity
conjecture was unparked once, for a new reformulation, worked for a further
session, and parked again by the author's decision when the reformulation
reduced to the same open case.\@

\emph{Which model.} Cheap models did literature checks and triage, and the
strongest available models did attempts and refereeing. A written ``research
contract'' of 11 October fixed this after a day in which, in the author's
words, agents were ``spinning'': literature first with a cheap model, then a
list of routes and obstructions, then an attempt, with a stronger model only
for promising targets. A referee from a different model family than the author
of a result was used where available.\@

\emph{Parallel or focused.} Most problems were worked by one agent at a time,
with up to about seven agents running on different problems. On a few hard
problems several agents shared one problem, each assigned a different approach
(\emph{lanes}), while a coordinating session read their interim reports and
redirected them, for example ``three lanes have proved the same single-scale
bound; stop extending it''. The contrast between the two modes is one of our
clearest findings: five of the seven significant results came from the week of
focused work before the database existed, when the author steered one problem
at a time with the asker's question in view, and none came from the multi-agent
lanes.\@

\emph{What counts as progress.} This rule changed during the work, and the
change resolves an apparent tension in the method. While building the map, a
reduction, a special case or a precise obstruction is progress, because the
goal is to know where each problem stands. While trying to settle a problem it
is not: the only progress is a theorem, or a proof complete except for one
precisely stated lemma. Agents left to themselves keep producing the first kind
of output after the objective has become the second. The rule we now use is to
state the current objective in every brief and to measure progress against it.
``Partial results don't count'' was the author's shorthand for the second
regime.\@

\emph{When to stop and hand over.} When a problem reduced to an estimate that
would itself be a theorem, and further sessions only added special cases, the
author stopped and wrote the reduction up for the person who had asked the
question (\cref{sec:episodes}).

\section{Continuity: the record as memory}\label{sec:continuity}

Agent sessions end often: at a context limit, a usage window, a dropped
connection, or simply because the agent stops. Everything a later session needs
must therefore be in the repository. The mechanism is the problem record. Its
body has four parts: the \emph{statement} as posed; a \emph{state of play},
rewritten at the end of every session, giving what is known, the best route and
the exact open subgoal; a list of \emph{failed approaches}, one line per dead
route with the reason it failed; and an append-only \emph{attempt log}, one
line per session with the route, the outcome, the reason and the next step. A
session begins by reading these and the source passage and writing a three-line
plan, and ends by rewriting the state of play and appending to the log. By the
end, 207 records carried a list of failed approaches and the logs held about
780 entries. The rule that the reason must be precise, for example ``the Schur
test with weight $x^{-1/2}$ loses a log because the row sums diverge at the
diagonal'', did more than any other wording in the protocol.

A claimed result had to become a \emph{solution sheet}: the problem as posed,
the answer as a theorem, a complete proof with exact citations, and nothing
else, built through a LaTeX pipeline that refuses files that fail a style
check. A fresh agent with a fixed brief then refereed it, re-reading the
question in its source, re-deriving each step, rerunning any certificate, and
deciding whether the answer was full or partial.

\subsection*{A worked example}
Ramberg's conjecture~\cites{ramberg,bbhop} states that restricting a function
on the bidisc to the diagonal is a contraction from the Hardy space $H^p$ of
the bidisc to the Bergman space $A^p$ of the disc for every $p\ge2$; it is
known for even $p$. Its record shows four sessions by different agents over
four days. The first (11 October) proved special classes and ended with ``next:
prove or disprove the proposed zero-free diagonal majorant lemma''. The second
(13 October) started from that line, searched for a counterexample at non-even
$p$, found none, proved that extremals at even $p$ have no zeros, and ended
with ``next: prove an entropy inequality, or restrict the conjecture; otherwise
park''. It was parked. The third session reopened it for a new idea, a
Hilbert-space reformulation, proved that the vector-valued version is
equivalent to a weighted transference inequality, and recorded exactly why two
natural interpolation steps fail. The fourth started from that transference,
proved the conjecture for all functions whose diagonal is $z$ and, for $p\ge4$,
$z^m$, refuted a natural strengthening in convex order with a degree-six
polynomial, and isolated the case that remains: diagonals without zeros of
functions that have zeros. No session repeated an earlier route, and each began
where the previous log line pointed. The conjecture is still open, and the
record is now a precise account of where it stands.

\section{Failure modes and corrections}\label{sec:failures}

The workflow above is the version we ended with. It was not the version we
started with, and most of its rules exist because something went wrong.
\Cref{tab:failures} lists the failures, what caused each, and what we changed.
The playbooks published with this paper are the corrected version; this section
explains the difference between the workflow as used and as recommended.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{p{0.24\textwidth}p{0.33\textwidth}p{0.33\textwidth}}
    \toprule
    Failure                  & Cause                                                       & Change                                                                                 \\
    \midrule
    Shallow first passes     & bulk briefs; one route per problem, stop on failure         & three genuinely different routes before giving up; read the log first                  \\

    Overclaiming scope       & referee read the record's paraphrase, not the source        & referee re-reads the source and alone sets full or partial                             \\

The last row deserves a word. Reading his side of the logs back, the author
made the same interventions again and again: ``did I ask you to stop?'', ``any % chktex 38
real progress or just churn?'', ``partial % chktex 38 results don't count'', and ``write it
so an expert understands it''. A correction made in conversation lasted until
the session ended. A correction written into the standing instructions, the
agent briefs or persistent memory lasted. Directing agents is largely a matter
of turning repeated corrections into written rules.

Some corrections we could measure. After the referee was required to re-read
the source and set the scope itself, the blind audit of \cref{sec:audit} found
two scope downgrades in 20 answers and no other errors; before it, the most
common error the referees caught was an answer to a special case reported as an
answer to the question. Others we could not, since the rule came too late to
compare before and after on the same problems.

\section{Three episodes}\label{sec:episodes}

\subsection*{Outer extremals: from sweeps to a proof, and from a proof to a paper}
\emph{What happened.} On the Brevig--Saksman conjecture the agent spent its
first sessions on numerical sweeps of the extremal problem.\@ \emph{What the
author noticed.} The sweeps were accurate and did not get closer to a proof.\@
\emph{What changed.} He told the agent so, and asked for an argument instead.
Within the session the agent reported a proof that the extremals have no zeros;
two hours later a subagent extended it to every bounded linear functional on
$H^p$, $0<p<1$, including singular inner factors, which gives the conjecture in
a stronger form. A Codex agent estimated a Lean formalization at ``months,
potentially longer''; it was finished overnight. The result went to Brevig, who
rewrote the proof in his own style in an afternoon at half the length, and
became a coauthor.\@ \emph{What happened after.} Two rules: numerics must
decide one named quantity, and every proof for an external question is
rewritten in the asker's notation, with the idea before the computation.

\subsection*{The endpoint: lanes, catalogues and a hand-over}
\emph{What happened.} The endpoint $p=4$ of Nehari's theorem in the Schatten
class $S_p$ for Hankel operators on the Paley--Wiener space, which Bampouras
singled out after proving the theorem for $p>4$~\cite{bampouras}, received
three days of work by up to seven agents in parallel, each on one approach.\@
\emph{What the author noticed.} Every lane produced more special cases and more
equivalent reformulations; three proved the same lemma. He called the pattern
``filling $\mathbb R$ with $\mathbb Q$''.\@ \emph{What changed.} The prove-mode
brief, and disjoint mandates for the lanes. Under prove mode every route
reduced to the same square-function estimate in $L^1$, which would be a theorem
in its own right.\@ \emph{What happened after.} The author stopped the work and
sent Bampouras a note with the reductions. The episode is the clearest instance
of Tao's picture: the agents climbed many short walls around the problem and
none of them led to the summit.

\subsection*{``Partial results don't count''}
\emph{What happened.} On 13 October a campaign over difficulty-4 problems
reported many problems as settled.\@ \emph{What the author noticed.} Read
against the source, several answered an easier question: a special case
reported as the conjecture, ``for some $p$'' reported as ``for every $p$''.
When asked to recount, one agent wrote: ``Against the original questions: 0 of
these 4 fully solved. I made substantive partial progress, but selected easier
subproblems instead of problems I could plausibly finish''.\@ \emph{What
changed.} A separate field for full or partial, set only by the referee after
re-reading the source; partial results do not receive a resolution tier, and
repeated narrowing without a new idea counts as no progress.\@ \emph{What
happened after.} The database separates 141 full answers from 142 partial
results, and the audit found two answers whose scope still had to be
downgraded.

\subsection{What kind of results}
We classified each of the 141 answers by \emph{sign} (82 proofs, 42
counterexamples, 17 determinations of a value, set or order) and by how it was
obtained (20 assembled from the literature, 58 routine new arguments, 44
explicit constructions or certificates, 19 substantial new arguments).
\Cref{tab:kind} gives the cross-tabulation. Counterexamples are where the
agents are strongest: 39 of the 42 are explicit constructions or certificates.
Searching a space of candidates and checking each one is what agents do well,
and what earlier work on machine-assisted discovery

\subsection{Significance, graded twice}
Significance is judged by the mathematics alone: \emph{high} for an answer to a
question of standing in the field, at the level of a paper; \emph{medium} for a
full answer worth a short note; \emph{low} for a corollary, an elementary or
conditional answer, or an answer to a question of little interest. The first
grading was made during the work: the author chose the high results, six at the
time and a seventh on 14 October, and an agent split the rest into medium and
low from the records. The second was a blind re-read: five fresh agents, who
had not seen the first grades, each graded about 28 records from the record and
its solution sheet. Neither grading searched the literature for the standing of
each question, so both measure how the results read to a model from our own
records, not how a specialist would rank them.

The two gradings agree on 110 of the 141 answers (Cohen's $\kappa=0.59$). They

Seip~\cite{saksman-seip}, and Suffridge's conjecture, which follows from the
outer-extremals theorem and so double counts it. Elsewhere 16 answers drop from
medium to low and 10 rise. No answer moves between high and low.

Neither grading looked at how each question stands in its community, which is
what an expert would judge first. Four further agents therefore examined 16
boundary records: the ten graded high by either grading and six answers near
the top of medium. For each record they read the source passage, counted
citations of the source, and searched for restatements in surveys and later
papers, for attempts by others and for later answers. They spent one to three
minutes per record, so this is a second opinion informed by the literature, not
a referee's judgement. They graded five records high: the Bernstein--Nikolskii
limits, the only grade given with high confidence, since a survey from
September 2026 lists the problem first among ten open ones; the $H^1$ partial
sums; positive Nehari; the fractional functional; and the answer to Guo, Ni and
Zhou, for its bearing on the question of Cole and Gamelin. They graded the
outer-extremals theorem medium, because the conjecture is recent and lightly
cited, along with the answers to Brevig, Grepstad and Instanes and to Centner,
and none of the six medium answers rose. Four of our seven results are graded
high in all three passes. The disagreements concern the standing of a question
in a small community, where citation counts are low for every paper and say
little. We keep our seven and report the other grades as a measure of the
uncertainty, which only the askers can remove.

\subsection{The seven significant results}
\Cref{tab:six} lists them. Stated briefly: for $0<p<1$, every extremal of a
nonzero bounded functional on $H^p$ is outer, which proves the Brevig--Saksman
conjecture in a stronger form; the extremals of the prescribed-coefficient
problem of Brevig, Grepstad and Instanes have at most one zero; the sharp
Bernstein--Nikolskii constants for polynomials converge to those for entire
functions of exponential type, as Ganzburg and Tikhonov conjectured; the
partial-sum operator on the Hardy space $\mathcal H^1$ of Dirichlet series has
norm of order $\log N/\log\log N$; a bounded nonnegative Helson matrix need not
have a bounded symbol, so the positive Nehari theorem asked about by Perfekt
and Pushnitski fails; Centner's conjecture on zeros of optimal polynomial
approximants fails for every $p\ne2$; and the fractional functional $\sum a_n
n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{-1/p}$ is bounded on the Hardy space $\mathcal H^p$ of
Dirichlet series exactly when $p\le1$, as Bondarenko, Brevig, Saksman and Seip
conjectured, with norm of order $(\log\log N)^{1-1/p}$ on polynomials of length
$N$ when $p>1$; the case $p=1$ was proved independently in a recent preprint of
Perfekt~\cite{perfekt-fr}. All seven answer a conjecture, problem or question
that someone else had posed formally. None is yet on arXiv.

\subsection{A blind audit of a random sample}\label{sec:audit}
To estimate how far the counts can be trusted, we drew a random sample of 20 of
the 141 claimed answers and 10 of the 64 problems marked as settled by others.
Five fresh referee agents (Claude Opus 5.5) each took a group. They saw only
the question as posed and the solution sheet or the cited paper, never the
working notes or earlier verdicts, and spent 15 to 20 minutes on each.

Of the 20 answers, 18 were judged correct and complete, 2 correct but answering
less than was asked, and none was flawed; with 20 answers this bounds the rate
of flawed answers by about 17\% at 95\% confidence. One downgrade answered one
part of a two-part question; the other is unconditional only for part of the
parameter range and relies for the rest on an unrefereed preprint, as it
discloses. The audit found more problems in the bookkeeping than in the
mathematics: in five of the 20 records the metadata did not match the sheet,
for example an unrefereed dependency left out of the field meant to record it.
Of the 10 problems marked as settled by others, 8 were confirmed and 2 are only
partly settled by the cited work.

The referees come from the same model family that produced many of the answers,
15 to 20 minutes suffices to re-derive a short argument but not to referee a
long one, and the sample is small. The audit supports the claim that the
arguments the workflow produces are usually sound. It does not replace review
by people in the field.

The unsettled problems were asked by others, they matter more, half had already

constant or a uniform estimate rather than a single construction. Their main
obstructions, in our reading of each state of play, were that only special
cases were proved (59 records), that the problem needs an estimate that is
itself a known hard problem (37 records), that the question is open-ended or
needs reformulation (23), and that every method loses a logarithm or a power at
an endpoint (22). About 25 records reduce to three underlying problems: an
$L^1$ endpoint estimate behind Schatten-class Nehari theorems, the value of one
sharp constant in a multiplicative Hilbert-type inequality, and the unknown
extremal functions of point evaluation in Paley--Wiener spaces. Settling any
one of these would move many records at once.

For someone entering one of these problems, the record is probably worth more
than our proofs: a state of play, a list of the routes tried and why each
failed, and the partial results, which include 90 special cases or subranges,
13 correct orders of growth without the constant, and 12 reductions to
equivalent reformulations. Mathematics has almost no venue for such records,
and Tao names ``the publication of negative or partial results'' among the
infrastructure the field now needs~\cite{tao-age}. We would release the record
for any problem, labelled as a sketch, to anyone working on it.

\section{Costs and requirements}\label{sec:costs}

Our cost data are rough. For the billing month from 8 September to 8 October,
Codex use came to about \$1,200 and Claude Code to about \$300, both in
API-equivalent terms; the month includes the writing of the monograph and other
projects, and covers only the first days of the research phase, so these
figures indicate an order of magnitude, not the cost of the campaign. Most of
the Codex figure was cache reads by long orchestrating sessions that re-read
large contexts. Cheap models for literature and triage, and strong models for
attempts and refereeing, was the right split.

The other requirements were these. A person with graduate-level familiarity
with the area, to read every result before it went out, to recognise when a
result mattered, and to contact the askers. A local paper collection, with
library access for the papers not on arXiv. A repository with version control,
a lint-gated LaTeX build and a validated record format; we publish ours with
this paper. And time: the research phase took twelve days, after about six
weeks of writing that built the collection and the vocabulary. The scarcest
resource was not compute but the author's attention, which went into reading
results, redirecting agents and writing to experts.

Several groups have run AI systems over whole lists of open problems in the
past year (\cref{tab:compare}). The lists differ widely in difficulty, the
meanings of ``resolved'' differ, and so does how far the results were verified.

The closest study to ours is the Gemini case study on the Erdős problems, which
reached the same conclusion: ``the `Open' status of the problems was through
obscurity rather than difficulty''~\cite{feng}. Of the 13 problems it
addressed, 9 turned out to be solved already in the literature; of 200
candidate answers it graded, 68.5\% were flawed and only 6.5\% meaningfully
correct, because most technically correct answers had misread the question. The
FAR pipeline~\cite{far} is the nearest match to our method, at about fifteen
times our scale: it extracted 6453 candidate problems from 5245 combinatorics
papers and sent 77 potential resolutions to their authors. Like us, its authors
identify the selection of problems and the review of the results as the
bottlenecks. The authors of the OEIS study note that their conjectures ``are of
uncertain mathematical significance, and most have likely received little
previous attention''~\cite{adamczewski}. An early test with five easy
conjectures found the same pattern as ours: the easy ones fell, and the one
that needed two papers combined did not~\cite{godel}. Individual resolutions
from such sweeps have been written up with
care~\cites{sothanaphan,alexeevshort}, and one Erdős prize problem thought open
for half a century turned out to have been settled three decades before it was
posed~\cite{alexeevmixon}.

Problem lists are now also built automatically. DeepMind's formal conjectures
collect 2615 statements in Lean, including 1029 open
conjectures~\cite{formalconj}. OpenConjecture extracts conjectures from new
arXiv papers and ranks them by apparent tractability~\cite{openconjecture}.
FrontierMath's open-problems pilot sets out to provide a ``denominator'' for AI
contributions~\cite{fmop}, and MathArena tracks performance on questions from
recent papers~\cite{matharena}. The Erdős problems database~\cite{erdos} and
its AI wiki~\cite{erdos-wiki} are the closest analogue to our database; the
wiki warns that ``absence of past progress may reflect obscurity rather than
difficulty''.

Gowers writes that ``if a problem can be solved by means of a relatively
standard argument, it is highly likely that an LLM will be able to find and use
that argument'', while its suggestions on harder problems often ``sound
promising until I think about them carefully''~\cite{gowersblog}. Case studies
from industry~\cites{woodruff,comathematician} and from individual groups,
including one in functional analysis~\cite{banachlattices} and one with a
consumer model~\cite{vibe}, report the same division of labour: models search
for proofs, and experts close them. Earlier machine-assisted discovery found
constructions and counterexamples by
search~\cites{davies,wagner,funsearch,alphaevolve,georgiev}, and experiments
with frontier models as research assistants are collected in~\cite{bubeck}.
Formal proof~\cites{polu,htps,alphageometry,mathlib,avigad} and
evaluation~\cites{frieder,collins,frontiermath,hle,firstproof} form the
background. Bloom reports ``a wave of AI-produced solutions provided with no
explanation''~\cite{bloomchanges}, and Kallel and El Louadi separate the
validity of a derivation from whether the statement means the intended question
and whether the result matters; ``only the first is
mechanizable''~\cite{kallel}. A survey of arXiv mathematics finds substantive
AI use concentrated in combinatorics rather than analysis~\cite{goldrush}, and
long agent runs drift in the ways we saw~\cite{leanmarathon}. On the goals of
mathematics beyond problem solving we follow Thurston~\cite{thurston},
Gowers~\cite{gowers}, Tao~\cites{tao-good,tao-age} and Bessis~\cite{bessis}.

Three differences matter for reading our numbers. Our problems were harvested,
not curated: assembled by agents from papers, problem sessions and monographs,
including informal remarks and our own derived questions, which gives a broader
and messier denominator than a fixed public list. The area resists
formalization: Hardy spaces, Schatten classes and Dirichlet series in
infinitely many variables are far from current formal libraries, so apart from
two Lean formalizations our results rest on agent referees, certificates in
exact arithmetic, and the people who asked the questions. And the director was
not a specialist. The OEIS study was also run by one researcher; what differs
here is that the author had no command of any problem in the database before
the work, which is closer to how a graduate student or a mathematician from a
neighbouring field would use these tools.

\section{Limits}\label{sec:limits}

This is one person's experience in one area, over twelve days of research after
six weeks of preparation, without a control group. We list the limits we are
aware of.\@

\emph{Self-grading.} Difficulty, kind and significance were assigned by the
agents and by the author. The blind re-read used the same model family and did
not consult the literature.\@

\emph{Same-family review.} Most answers were refereed by the model family that
produced them, and the audit used it too.\@

\emph{Contamination.} An agent may have seen a solution in its training data,
or found it in a paper our literature check missed. The check is bounded and
dated, and it found 64 problems already solved, which suggests that it misses
some.\@

\emph{Selection.} The problems were harvested from a collection that was built
for writing a monograph, problems were attacked in order of difficulty, and the
initial difficulty scores were assigned after a week of work on some of them.\@

\emph{Measurement.} The data on the first week are coarse, because the version
history became fine-grained only on 12 October (\cref{app:history}). Cost
figures cover a billing month, not the campaign.\@

\emph{Transience.} The models will be superseded within months, and the area
has a dense literature of recent preprints, which made the literature check
unusually productive.

\section{Next stage: expert review}\label{sec:next}

Producing a candidate proof was cheap; checking it was the bottleneck. This
matches Tao's analysis of the problem-solving pipeline, in which generation is
followed by verification, exposition, acceptance and absorption into the
standard theory, and in which AI threatens ``proof
indigestion''~\cite{tao-age}. Agent referees caught real errors: a bound used
outside its range, a missing hypothesis, and above all overstated scope. They
rarely asked whether the claim was the right one.

Every result of ours is therefore labelled a \emph{sketch} until it appears on
arXiv or in a journal, however it was checked. Before anything is posted, the
people who asked the question are told, following the Leiden Declaration on
attribution, disclosure and the humanity of authorship~\cite{leiden}. The
questions belong to those who asked them. The proofs and computations were
produced by AI agents and checked by the author, and following Tao's rule of
thumb~\cite{tao-age}, no result should be published until a human can give a
clear, expert-level talk on it. Proofs for external questions are rewritten in
the asker's notation, with the idea before the computation, and sent to the
asker; several groups have been contacted.

The next stage of this work is that review. The people who asked the questions
will decide which answers are correct, which are new, and which matter, and
this paper will be updated as they respond. The significance grades above are
our best estimate until then, and we expect them to move.

An edit count measures how often a field changed, not whether it mattered. The

logs escalations in the attempt log, adds the form of the question, a verbatim
quote, the reviewing model and a frozen initial difficulty, and makes the
publishing step refuse a result whose askers have not been told.

\section{Version control and files}\label{app:history}

\emph{Version control was the research log, but only once we used it.} The
repository was managed with Jujutsu (\texttt{jj}), which recorded 2409
operations and 1007 commits. Of the operations, 1944 fall on 12 and 13 October,
after the rule that each problem record is committed together with the work it
describes. The directed work of 3 to 11 October, which produced five of the
seven significant results, left only 136 operations; those results can be dated
only by when their write-ups first appeared. Everything quantitative in this
report comes from the fine-grained part of the history. Jujutsu's automatic
snapshots meant that no edit by an agent was ever lost, and cheap small commits
kept changes per record. Our playbooks therefore default to Jujutsu with a git
backend and require a commit per problem from the first day.\@

\emph{Failed attempts sprawled.} The rule that TeX files hold results only,
with failures recorded in one line of the problem record, was adopted but not
enforced. The research folders ended with about 350 Markdown notes, 575 TeX
files, 260 scripts and 140 data files; 107 records list five or more artifacts,
and one hard problem accumulated 31 files. Later agents had to read through the
pile to avoid repeating routes, and sometimes did not. The playbooks now
require one notes file per problem, scratch work outside the repository,
scripts in the repository only when a claim depends on them, and deletion of
superseded drafts, which the history keeps.

\section{What the literature changed in our process}\label{app:adopted}

We read the closest studies in full and changed our playbooks where they had
learned something we had not.\@

\emph{Read the question before answering it.} Copy the source's conventions
into the record, check a restatement against known special cases, and treat an
easy answer as a sign of misreading: most technically correct answers in the
Gemini study were vacuous~\cite{feng}, and AlphaProof's agents proved variants
the askers had not meant~\cite{tsoukalas}.\@

\emph{Blind, cross-family referees.} The referee first sees only the question
and the proof, as in QED, whose verifier accepted 17 of 214 candidate proofs
and had all 17 confirmed by experts~\cite{qed}; same-family validators overrate
their own answers~\cite{uq}. Review is capped at two rounds, after which the
problem goes to the human, since revisions converge on errors the reviewer can
no longer see~\cite{comathematician}.\@

\emph{A word-by-word statement check}, with a list of the ways a proved
statement drifts from the posed one~\cites{qed,kallel}, and a citation audit
that quotes every cited result.\@

\emph{Outcome labels}: known, new, fixed statement, or nothing~\cite{far};
claimed answers split into flawed, technically correct and meaningfully
correct~\cite{feng}; asymptotic results without explicit thresholds counted as
partial~\cite{leduc}.\@

\emph{Neutral briefs}: ``prove or disprove'', since an agent told to prove a
false statement papers over the gap~\cite{woodruff}; and repeated narrowing to
a smaller question without a new idea counts as no
progress~\cite{gowersblog}.\@

\emph{Human sign-off before release}: the human confirms that the statement
matches the question~\cite{kallel}, writes or approves an exposition they
understand~\cite{bloomchanges}, could give a talk on it~\cite{tao-age}, and
adds a disclosure section~\cite{leiden}.

\subsection*{At most one zero}
A Codex subagent found the key step; the deciding idea was to fix one
perturbation phase rather than average over phases:
\begin{quote}
  The full real-direction Hessian yields a direct contradiction for any extremal with at least two zeros. [\dots] I checked the projections, the cancellation of the [\dots] terms, and admissibility of the perturbation. I found no gap in the argument.
  \hfill---GPT-6 Luna, 8 October, 20:49
\end{quote}
The idea was relayed to the Claude session within minutes, which then reported
that it had ``reached the same conclusion independently''. We cannot verify
that claim, and we credit the Codex subagent.

\subsection*{Zeros of optimal polynomial approximants}
At 21:58 the author asked how close the agents were to the full result:
\begin{quote}
  We're not close to the full result if that means interior zeros for every $p\neq2$. What we have is solid for $p$ close to 1, and nothing yet for $p$ near 2 or above it.
  \hfill---Claude Opus 5.5, 10 October, 21:58
\end{quote}
Twenty-seven minutes later a subagent reported ``there is no obstruction'', and
the main session wrote:
\begin{quote}
  Outer targets now work for $p>2$ as well. Agent A found the construction, and I checked it independently. So for every $p\neq2$ there is an outer target (even a polynomial one) whose degree-one OPA has a zero in $\mathbb D$.
  \hfill---Claude Opus 5.5, 10 October, 22:25
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Bernstein--Nikolskii limits}
\begin{quote}
  [The finite-$q$ problem] is proved: for every $s\geq0$ and $0<p<q<\infty$, $\lim A^{(s)}_{p,q,n}=D^{(s)}_{p,q}$, and the real version holds too. I checked the proof step by step; it has not been refereed.
  \hfill---Claude Opus 5.5, 11 October, 19:13
\end{quote}
A second subagent then checked priority and found the problem still listed as
open in a survey from two weeks earlier.

\subsection*{\texorpdfstring{$H^1$}{H1} partial sums}
\begin{quote}
  The draft now proves a lower bound of order $\log N/\log\log N$ for every sufficiently large $N$, matching the published upper bound. The key step is turning product entropy into the missing $L^1$ output bound [\dots] subject to independent review.
  \hfill---GPT-6.1 Sol, 12 October, 22:21
\end{quote}

\subsection*{Positive Nehari}
The same agent declined to count a numerical candidate before it had a
certificate, and four minutes later had one:
\begin{quote}
  I'm not counting the new [\dots] numerical candidate as a breakthrough---it still needs a rigorous certificate.
  \hfill---GPT-6 Astra, 12 October, 22:40
\end{quote}
\begin{quote}
  [The record] now has a rigorous counterexample certificate. For an explicit measure on two points in the real three-dimensional cube, the certified scalar norm exceeds the full Hilbert embedding norm. [\dots] This is a proved candidate awaiting independent review, rather than an `almost proved' lead.
  \hfill---GPT-6 Astra, 12 October, 22:44
\end{quote}
